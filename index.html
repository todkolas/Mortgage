Part 1

Concepts you need first

Before either strategy makes sense, you need a few building blocks.

What is a HELOC?

HELOC stands for Home Equity Line of Credit. Think of it like a credit card backed by your house.

  • The bank gives you a credit limit (typically up to 65–80% of home value minus your mortgage balance).
  • You can borrow, repay, and re-borrow as many times as you want without re-applying.
  • You only pay interest on the amount you've actually borrowed (the balance).
  • Interest is calculated daily on the current balance.
  • The interest rate is variable — it changes when prime rate changes.
Example
  • Home value: $700,000
  • Existing mortgage: $400,000
  • Bank allows 80% loan-to-value: $700,000 × 0.80 = $560,000
  • HELOC limit: $560,000 − $400,000 = $160,000 available to borrow

What is a readvanceable mortgage?

A regular mortgage and a HELOC bundled together, where they automatically rebalance.

The key feature: as you pay down mortgage principal, the HELOC limit automatically grows by the same amount. This is essential for the Smith Manoeuvre.

Example
  • Month 0: Mortgage $500,000, HELOC limit $0
  • Month 12: You've paid down $12,000 of mortgage principal
  • Month 12: Mortgage $488,000, HELOC limit becomes $12,000

You can now borrow up to $12,000 from the HELOC without applying for anything.

Common Canadian products: Scotia Total Equity Plan (STEP), TD Home Equity FlexLine, National Bank All-in-One, Manulife One.

How HELOC interest is calculated

Daily, on outstanding balance.

Formula: balance × annual_rate × (days / 365)

Example

$10,000 balance at 6% for 30 days:
$10,000 × 0.06 × (30/365) = $49.32

This is why "parking" cash in a HELOC reduces interest — every day the balance is lower, you pay less.

What "tax deductible" actually means

In Canada, your tax bill = (your income) × (your tax rate). A "tax deduction" reduces the income figure.

Example
  • You earn $100,000
  • Marginal tax rate: 40%
  • You owe roughly $30,000 in tax
  • You get a $5,000 deduction → income becomes $95,000
  • New tax: roughly $28,000
  • You save $2,000 ($5,000 × 40%)

The savings = deduction × marginal tax rate. Higher tax bracket = more value from the deduction.

Why mortgage interest isn't deductible (but investment loan interest is)

Canadian rule (Income Tax Act s. 20(1)(c)):

  • Money you borrowed to live in (your house) → interest is NOT deductible
  • Money you borrowed to earn income (investments paying dividends/interest) → interest IS deductible

This single rule is the entire foundation of the Smith Manoeuvre.

What a marginal tax rate is

The rate that applies to your next dollar of income — not your average rate.

Ontario, 2025 (combined federal + provincial), approximate brackets:

  • $0–53K: ~20%
  • $53–106K: ~30%
  • $106–165K: ~37%
  • $165–235K: ~43%
  • $235K+: ~48–53%

If you earn $150,000, your marginal rate is ~37–43%. Every extra dollar of income is taxed at that rate, and every dollar of deduction saves you that rate in tax.

What a dividend ETF is and why it matters

  • ETF = Exchange Traded Fund. A basket of stocks bought as one unit.
  • Dividend ETF = an ETF holding companies that pay regular cash dividends.
  • Common Canadian examples: VDY, XEI, ZDV, CDZ.
  • Typical dividend yield: 3–5% per year (paid quarterly as cash).

Why it matters for the Smith Manoeuvre: the CRA requires that borrowed money produce income (not just price appreciation) for the interest to be deductible. Dividend-paying ETFs reliably produce income.

Part 2

HELOC velocity banking

The strategy that goes viral on YouTube. Mostly hype.

The pitch

"Use your HELOC as a chequing account. Park your paycheck in it. Pay all your bills from it. Periodically pay down the mortgage with HELOC funds. Pay off your 25-year mortgage in 7 years!"

How it's supposed to work, step by step

Setup
  • Mortgage: $400,000 at 5%
  • HELOC: $30,000 limit at 6%
  • Monthly take-home: $6,000
  • Monthly expenses: $4,500
  • Monthly surplus: $1,500
Step 1

Take a chunk from HELOC, smash the mortgage

Borrow $20,000 from HELOC. Apply directly to mortgage principal.
Now: Mortgage = $380,000, HELOC balance = $20,000

Step 2

Use HELOC as your primary bank account

  • Paycheck of $6,000 deposited into HELOC → balance drops to $14,000
  • Throughout the month, pay all bills from HELOC → balance creeps back up to ~$18,500
  • Net: HELOC reduced by $1,500 (your monthly surplus)
Step 3

Repeat

Each month, HELOC balance net-drops by $1,500. After ~13 months, the $20,000 lump is paid off.

Step 4

Repeat the whole cycle

Take another $20,000 from HELOC, smash mortgage, repeat.

Where the savings actually come from

Source 1: "Parking your paycheck reduces daily interest"

Your $6,000 paycheck sits in the HELOC for ~15 days on average before you've spent it on bills.

$6,000 × 6% × (15/365) = $14.79 per month
Annual: ~$177
Over 25 years: ~$4,425

That's all this trick saves. About four grand over the life of the mortgage.

Source 2: "Surplus paying down debt faster"

Your $1,500/month surplus is now applied to debt instead of sitting in chequing. But this works whether you use a HELOC or just send the $1,500 directly to your mortgage as an extra payment.

The HELOC adds nothing to this — except complexity and risk.

Why it often makes the math worse

HELOC rate (6%) is usually higher than mortgage rate (5%).

Carrying $20,000 on a 6% HELOC instead of a 5% mortgage costs you an extra:
$20,000 × 1% = $200/year in extra interest

  • You save $177/year from the parking trick
  • You pay $200/year extra in rate spread
  • Net: −$23/year. You're actually losing money.

The strategy only "works" because it forces discipline you weren't applying before.

Who actually does this

  • People who can't make extra payments without artificial constraints
  • People who watch finance YouTubers
  • People with HELOC rate ≤ mortgage rate (rare in Canada in normal rate environments)
Honest verdict

Skip it. Set up biweekly accelerated payments and apply your surplus directly to mortgage. Same outcome, zero rate-spread loss, zero risk of overspending.

Part 3

Smith Manoeuvre

A legitimate Canadian wealth-building strategy. Not for everyone.

The goal in one sentence

Convert your mortgage into a tax-deductible investment loan, while building a parallel investment portfolio over 20–30 years.

The legal foundation

Singleton v. Canada (Supreme Court, 2001). The court ruled that how you arrange your finances matters more than the order of cash flows. If you deliberately structure things so borrowed money is used for investment, the interest is deductible — even if the cash you "freed up" went toward your house.

This is settled law. It's not a loophole. The CRA accepts it when done correctly.

Required ingredients

  1. Readvanceable mortgage (HELOC limit auto-grows as you pay principal)
  2. Discipline to invest every single month
  3. Non-registered investment account (not TFSA, not RRSP — those have separate tax rules)
  4. Income-producing investments (dividend ETFs, REITs, bond funds)
  5. Clean paper trail — separate HELOC sub-account used only for investing

The mechanics, step by step

Starting state
  • Home: $700,000
  • Readvanceable mortgage: $500,000 at 5%, 25-year amortization
  • Monthly payment: ~$2,910
  • Marginal tax rate: 40%
  • HELOC rate: 6%

Each month, you do this:

  1. Make your normal mortgage payment ($2,910).
  2. Some portion is principal (~$850 in month 1, growing each month). The HELOC limit grows by that amount.
  3. You borrow that exact principal amount from the HELOC and invest it in a dividend ETF.
  4. The HELOC interest accrues daily. You either let it capitalize or use dividends to cover it.
  5. Once a year, you tell your accountant the total HELOC interest paid. They claim it as a deduction.
  6. You get a tax refund worth (HELOC interest × your marginal rate).
  7. Optionally, apply the refund directly to mortgage principal — this accelerates everything.

Year-by-year evolution

Round numbers, no accelerator.

End of year 1
  • Mortgage paid down: ~$10,500
  • HELOC balance: ~$10,800
  • Investment portfolio: ~$11,000
  • HELOC interest paid: ~$330
  • Tax refund: $330 × 40% = ~$132
End of year 5
  • Mortgage paid down: ~$60,000
  • HELOC balance: ~$65,000
  • Investment portfolio: ~$78,000 (with 7% returns)
  • HELOC interest paid that year: ~$3,900
  • Tax savings that year: ~$1,560
End of year 15
  • Mortgage paid down: ~$240,000
  • HELOC balance: ~$280,000
  • Investment portfolio: ~$420,000
  • Annual HELOC interest: ~$16,800
  • Annual tax savings: ~$6,720
End of year 25 (mortgage fully paid off)
  • Mortgage: $0
  • HELOC balance: ~$500,000 (still tax-deductible)
  • Investment portfolio: ~$900,000–$1,200,000 (depends on returns)
  • Annual HELOC interest: ~$30,000
  • Annual tax savings: ~$12,000

Where the wealth actually comes from

  1. Leverage. You have ~$1,000,000 in stocks instead of $0 because you used borrowed money to invest for 25 years.
  2. Tax savings. Roughly $100,000–$150,000 in cumulative tax refunds over 25 years.
  3. Compounding. Money invested in year 1 has 25 years to grow.
  4. Dividends. Cash flow that helps cover HELOC interest.

The accelerator add-on

If you also apply each year's tax refund to the mortgage:

  • Mortgage gets paid off 3–7 years sooner
  • HELOC balance ends up the same (~$500K)
  • Investment portfolio ends up larger (more time to compound)

Risks (read these carefully)

  1. Market crash. Your portfolio can drop 40% while you still owe the full HELOC. Your net worth temporarily collapses. If you panic-sell, you lock in losses — strategy ruined.
  2. Interest rate spike. HELOCs are variable. Rate going from 6% to 12% doubles your interest cost.
  3. Job loss. You can't service the HELOC; you may have to liquidate investments at the worst possible moment.
  4. Loss of dividends. If your ETF stops paying dividends, the CRA could question the deduction.
  5. Discipline failure. Spending the HELOC on a vacation or car kills your tax-deduction status — and you've taken on debt for nothing.
  6. CRA audit. Co-mingling investment HELOC funds with personal funds in any way can disqualify the entire deduction. The paper trail must be airtight.

Who actually does this

  • High-income professionals (engineers, doctors, lawyers, senior corporate)
  • Household income $150,000+ with marginal tax rate 37%+
  • TFSA and RRSP already maxed (do those first)
  • 15+ years until retirement
  • Stable dual-income households with 6+ month emergency fund
  • Investors who've lived through a 30%+ crash without panic-selling

Required minimums to actually benefit

  • Income: $100,000 household minimum, $150,000+ ideal
  • Marginal rate: 30%+ (below this, the deduction value is too small to justify the risk)
  • Cash flow buffer: ability to absorb a 50% rate spike without distress
  • Emergency fund: 6 months expenses, kept entirely outside the strategy
  • Time: 15+ years for the compounding to do its work
Honest verdict

A real strategy that builds real wealth — for the right person. Most Canadians shouldn't do it. Those who genuinely fit the profile and stick with it for 20–30 years tend to end up significantly wealthier than they would have otherwise.

Part 5

Action checklist

Concrete steps if you're considering either path.

If you're considering HELOC velocity banking

Don't. Set up biweekly accelerated payments. Send any monthly surplus directly to mortgage as a prepayment. Done.

If you're considering the Smith Manoeuvre

  1. Confirm income and tax bracket make it worth doing (≥$100K household, ≥30% marginal rate)
  2. Max your TFSA and RRSP first — those are tax-free/tax-deferred and beat Smith Manoeuvre on a risk-adjusted basis
  3. Build 6 months emergency fund completely outside this plan
  4. Refinance into a readvanceable mortgage if you don't have one
  5. Talk to a fee-only financial planner — Ed Rempel is the recognized Canadian Smith Manoeuvre expert
  6. Open a dedicated HELOC sub-account used only for investing
  7. Open a non-registered investment account (separate from TFSA/RRSP)
  8. Choose a dividend ETF (VDY, XEI, ZDV, or similar)
  9. Automate the monthly process
  10. Keep meticulous records — every transaction documented separately for CRA
Part 6

Common questions

Quick answers to the questions that come up most often.

Can I do this with my TFSA or RRSP?

No. CRA disallows interest deductibility on borrowed money invested in registered accounts.

What if I sell my house?

The HELOC must be paid off at sale. The investments stay yours.

What if my dividend ETF cuts its dividend?

The deduction continues as long as there's a reasonable expectation of income. A temporary dividend cut is usually fine; complete elimination is risky.

Can I deduct HELOC interest if I use HELOC funds to renovate my house?

No. Renovating your primary residence is personal use. Only income-producing uses qualify.

What about a rental property HELOC?

Different strategy ("debt swap" or "cash damming"). Same legal principle, different mechanics.

Can I do this with a US-listed ETF?

Yes, but you'll deal with USD conversion, foreign withholding tax, and slightly more complex tax reporting.

This guide is educational. Consult a fee-only financial advisor and a CPA before implementing the Smith Manoeuvre. CRA rules can change.
